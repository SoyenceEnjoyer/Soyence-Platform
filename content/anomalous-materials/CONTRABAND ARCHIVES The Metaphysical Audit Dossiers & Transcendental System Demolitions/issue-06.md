### Historical Timeline of Global Argumentation Methods
Human civilizations developed distinct rules for intellectual disputation over two millennia:

1. **Eastern Logic & Debate Rules (Nyāya & Buddhist Pramāṇa) — 500 BCE – 200 BCE**  
   India's *Nyāya Sūtras* establish a rigorous 5-step debate method based on valid means of knowledge (*Pramāṇa*). Early Buddhist debaters focus on deconstructing opponents' claims by exposing inherent contradictions (*Prāsaṅgika*), pioneering early internal critique.
2. **Classical Western Dialectic & Syllogistic (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle) — 400 BCE – 300 BCE**  
   Socrates introduces the dialectic (testing definitions through cross-examination). Aristotle formalizes categorical logic and the Syllogism, shifting Western debate toward **deductive proofs and immediate evidence**.
3. **Patristic Polemics, Neoplatonism, & Islamic Kalām — 200 CE – 800 CE**  
   The debate shifts from isolated logic to entire cosmic models. Early Christian Fathers (Augustine, Athanasius, Maximos the Confessor) battle Neoplatonists and heretical sects by exposing the preconditions of truth and revelation. In the East, Islamic scholars formulate *Kalām* (rational defense of divine unity), laying the structural groundwork for **formal systemic disputation**.
4. **Medieval Scholastic Disputation (Thomas Aquinas & The Universities) — 1100 CE – 1400 CE**  
   Western disputation formalizes into university *Quaestiones*. Thinkers assume a shared, neutral ground of natural reason to build Classical Evidentialist proofs for God's existence (e.g., Aquinas's Five Ways).
5. **The Enlightenment & Autonomous Foundationalism (Descartes, Locke, Kant) — 1600s – 1800s**  
   Shift toward total human autonomy. Rationalists try to prove reality from bare human consciousness (*Cogito*), while Empiricists build on pure sensory observation. Both assume human reason can judge reality **without divine revelation**.
6. **The Presuppositional Shift (Cornelius Van Til & Transcendental Critique) — 1900s – Present**  
   Van Til demonstrates that neutral ground is a myth. Rather than arguing about isolated facts on shared neutral ground, debate shifts to **worldview vs. worldview**—testing which system provides the necessary preconditions for logic, science, and morality.
